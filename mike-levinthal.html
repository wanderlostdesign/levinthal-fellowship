Four decades in venture

Mike Levinthal trained as an engineer at Stanford, where he earned both his bachelor's and master's degrees, and then took an MBA at the Stanford Graduate School of Business. He went on to build a career in venture capital that has now spanned more than forty years.

He spent roughly two decades at Mayfield Fund, one of Silicon Valley's founding venture firms, rising to Managing Partner. During his time there the firm raised and managed six funds totaling about $1.4 billion. He also founded Mayfield Software Partners, a fund backed by a group of successful software entrepreneurs.

Companies he backed along the way include:

A teacher of founders

In the mid-1990s, Stanford's School of Engineering launched an experiment: a small cohort of students who would study technology ventures in the classroom and then work inside a startup. Mike was an early champion of the idea. He brought Mayfield Fund on board as its sponsor, which gave the program its name and anchored it in Silicon Valley.

Three decades later, the Mayfield Fellows Program is still one of Stanford's flagship entrepreneurship experiences, and its hundreds of alumni return as speakers and mentors. In 2001 the model expanded to UC Berkeley. Mike has also served on the advisory board of the Stanford Technology Ventures Program.

Levinthal Capital & angel investing

After moving to Utah, Mike founded Levinthal Capital, the family office through which he invests independently as Managing Partner. He has backed founders across both Silicon Valley and the Intermountain West. Many of those bets were consumer brands, which is a different playbook from the enterprise software he funded at Mayfield.

Why BYU, and why Mexico City

Mike lives in Park City, Utah, and serves on the BYU Marriott School's entrepreneurship advisory board. The Levinthal Fellowship brings the Mayfield model to BYU students, with an international twist. Fellows learn from Mike and his network around a seminar table, and some evenings that table is in his own home. They then test what they've learned at startups in Mexico City.

His seminar is built on discussion rather than lecture. Fellows read, prepare, and debate real decisions faced by founders and investors, and many of those decisions were made by the people sitting in the room as guests.
